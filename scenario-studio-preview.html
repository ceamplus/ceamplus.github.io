Marketing and research what-if analysis

A workspace for exploring possibilities.

Scenario Studio is a flexible projections and what-if workspace for exploring marketing and research decisions. Users can define assumptions, compare possible conditions, test alternatives, examine uncertainty, and identify what evidence would change a decision.

Future unscored Project Briefs may supply decision context alongside assessments, evidence, data, Analytics Lab findings and user-defined assumptions. An assessment alone does not automatically provide sufficient data for projections. This describes the proposed future workspace. Here, two fictional examples demonstrate transparent arithmetic and a written decision process, not the working Studio engine.

Scenario, projection, forecast and prediction
Scenario
A defined combination of assumptions, conditions, choices, and constraints.
Projection
A conditional result showing what could happen if the stated assumptions hold.
Forecast
An evidence-based estimate generated through an identified forecasting method using appropriate data.
Prediction
A claim about an outcome that will occur.

This preview uses scenarios, conditional projections and what-if analysis. Its illustrative arithmetic is neither a forecast nor a prediction. No likelihood is assigned to any scenario.

Example starting structures. Equal scrutiny.

Six ways to start a scenario.

These are example starting structures, not mandatory categories, rankings or recommendations. Future users should be able to name and define their own scenarios. A target case is a documented plan, not a preferred outcome.

01Baseline

What happens if current conditions continue?

02Conservative case

What happens under tighter resources, lower response, or slower progress?

03Target case

What happens if the stated plan performs according to its documented assumptions?

04Alternative strategy

What happens if a different channel, method, price, audience, or research design is used?

05Stress case

What happens if costs rise, participation falls, timing changes, demand weakens, or another important assumption fails?

06Custom scenario

What situation does the user want to define and compare?

Fictional marketing example

Juniper Service Co.: a three-month campaign

The invented local service business compares budget and channel options. Reach counts distinct people reached over three months, not repeated impressions. Responses count distinct people who respond, with at most one response per reached person. Leads are potential customers meeting defined qualification criteria; lead qualification is not modeled here, so responses are not labeled qualified leads.

Conversion means a specified action. In this simplified example, the conversion action is one completed purchase per converting respondent. Every row assumes 25% of responding people make that purchase, a $120 average revenue per purchase and a 40% gross margin. Purchases are transactions; sales count means the number of these transactions, while revenue is their dollar value. Repeat purchases are excluded.

Change one assumption: target-campaign response

For these fictional conditional projections, hold reach at 10,000 people, purchase conversion among responders at 25%, average revenue per purchase at $120, gross margin at 40% and budget at $1,200 over three months.

Fictional conditional projection at an assumed 2% response: 200 responses; 50 purchases (sales count); $6,000 revenue; $1,200 contribution after advertising spend.

Fictional static comparison: at 1% response, the same rule gives 100 responses, 25 purchases (sales count), $3,000 revenue and $0 contribution after advertising spend. Neither assumption is assigned a likelihood.

Evidence, capacity and attention still matter

Possible user-defined variables include advertising budget, channel allocation, audience reach, response, lead-qualification and purchase-conversion rates, average revenue per purchase, gross margin, customer acquisition cost, repeat-purchase assumptions, employee capacity, campaign duration, seasonality, consumer attention burden and evidence confidence.

For the fictional target illustration, budget divided by assumed purchases is $24 per purchase, rising to $48 at 1% response. It is only an advertising-spend-per-assumed-purchase ratio; new-customer identity, incrementality and total acquisition costs are not established. Check historical comparability, actual service capacity, consent, frequency and total work before interpreting any campaign. More sales or spend is not automatically a better choice. A different mix, delayed campaign, reduced activity or no change may be preferable.

Fictional research-planning example

A mixed-method study: recruitment and completion

An invented researcher compares recruitment channels, cost, timing and participation assumptions for the survey component of a mixed-method study. Invitations count distinct people invited once. Survey starts count invited people who begin the survey. The survey-start rate uses invitations as its denominator; it is not a completed-survey response rate. Every row assumes 80% of starts become completed responses, with 20% not completing.

Completed responses reach the defined end of the survey. Usable responses are the subset meeting documented eligibility and data-quality criteria after review; no usable-response rate or count is assumed here. Retained participants remain enrolled through a specified later study stage or follow-up; neither retention nor later attrition is modeled. Completed responses are not automatically usable responses or retained participants.

Recruitment plus incentive cost = $400 + completed responses × $10. Researcher time, interviews, accessibility support and other costs are excluded.

Fictional static comparison: at a 15% survey-start rate, the same rule gives 120 survey starts, 96 completed responses and $1,360 recruitment plus incentive cost. Lower cost here accompanies fewer completed responses and is not a preferred outcome. The illustrative incentive rule is not a recommendation about participant compensation.

Research quality cannot be inferred from counts

Possible variables include recruitment channels, invitations distributed, survey-start and completion rates, usable responses, later participant retention, interview participation, recruitment and incentive costs, collection period, researcher time, participant burden, attrition, missing data, accessibility, evidence confidence and scope changes.

Keep interview recruitment and survey completion separate. Review inclusion, consent, missingness, accessible participation, researcher capacity and participant time. This preview does not calculate statistical power, qualitative saturation, validity or a correct sample size. Those require appropriate methods and contextual evidence. A lower-resource design, revised scope, longer recruitment, pause or no study may be appropriate.

A shared 14-step workflow

The same proposed process applies to marketing and research. Open any step. Saving and a general workspace remain future capabilities.

  1. Define the question

    State the marketing or research decision, whose objective it serves and whether any change is necessary.

  2. Establish the baseline

    Record current conditions, the existing plan and the human interpretation before comparing alternatives. Preserve this starting point.

  3. Select the outcome of interest

    Choose relevant outcomes such as contribution after advertising spend, completed responses, workload or participant burden. Do not combine them into one universal score.

  4. Identify available evidence

    Record sources, dates, comparable populations, missing observations and conflicts. The examples above contain invented inputs, not measured evidence.

  5. Enter assumptions

    State units, period and definitions. Keep reach, responses, lead qualification, purchase conversion, survey starts, completion, usability, retention and cost separate from observations and from each other.

  6. Label uncertainty

    Explain what is unknown and why. An evidence-confidence note is not a probability of success. Do not invent a distribution or likelihood.

  7. Create alternative scenarios

    Use any useful starting structure or name a custom case. Include current conditions, delay, no change or a revised plan where relevant.

  8. Select variables to change

    Change one assumption to examine sensitivity, or document multiple changes when comparing a strategy. Keep other conditions visible.

  9. Calculate conditional projections

    Apply a stated arithmetic rule only to supplied assumptions. In the marketing example, reach × response rate gives responses; responses × purchase-conversion rate gives the purchase count. This is not a forecast.

  10. Examine sensitivity

    Switch the marketing response rate or research survey-start rate in the examples above. The result changes mechanically; the control supplies no evidence that either assumption is likely.

  11. Consider resources, people, and constraints

    Review capacity, attention, accessibility, time, participant burden, ethics, sustainability and recovery. Financial improvement alone cannot settle the decision.

  12. Establish thresholds and stop conditions

    Document who selected each threshold. Pause or revise if costs, burden or data quality breach the agreed conditions; no change may be preferable.

  13. Document what evidence would change the decision

    Identify comparable observations, disconfirming findings and affected people’s feedback that would justify continuing, delaying, declining, revising or reversing.

  14. Save the scenario for later comparison and revision

    A future platform may retain versions, original assumptions and observed outcomes. Saving is not available in this preview. No project or assessment record is created here.

Planned narration and written transcript

Script for a future recording, not a transcript of an existing video. All values are fictional and illustrative.

Scenario Studio is in development for the future CEAM+ Platform. It is a flexible marketing and research projections and what-if environment. This public preview uses fictional information, not a live customer workspace.

A scenario combines assumptions, conditions, choices and constraints. A conditional projection shows what follows if those assumptions hold. A forecast requires an identified method and appropriate evidence. These examples are not forecasts or predictions.

First, consider fictional Juniper Service Co. It is planning a three-month advertising campaign. Compare its current approach, a lower-cost campaign, a target campaign, a different channel mix, a higher-cost campaign and a weaker-response stress case. None is automatically preferred.

For the target example, assume ten thousand distinct people reached and two percent responding once. That gives two hundred responses. Lead qualification is not modeled, so these are not labeled qualified leads. Here, conversion means one purchase by a responding person. Assume twenty-five percent of responding people make that purchase. That gives fifty purchases, also called the sales count. At one hundred twenty dollars of revenue per purchase, revenue is six thousand dollars. Apply a forty percent gross margin and subtract twelve hundred dollars of advertising spend: twelve hundred dollars remain before other costs. This is not net profit or evidence of incremental campaign impact.

Now change only response to one percent. Responses fall to one hundred, purchases to twenty-five, revenue to three thousand dollars and contribution after advertising spend to zero. The arithmetic responds to the assumption. It does not tell us which response rate is likely. Check evidence, service capacity, attention burden and whether less advertising is sufficient.

Next, an invented researcher plans the survey component of a mixed-method study. Assume eight hundred people invited once, a twenty-five percent survey-start rate and eighty percent completion among starts. Two hundred people start and one hundred sixty complete the survey. At a fifteen percent survey-start rate, one hundred twenty start and ninety-six complete. Hold recruitment cost at four hundred dollars and the incentive at ten dollars per completed survey. Recruitment plus incentives total two thousand dollars in the first case and thirteen hundred sixty in the second. A completed response has reached the survey end; it is not automatically usable after eligibility and quality checks. A retained participant remains through a specified later stage or follow-up. Usability, later retention and later attrition are not modeled here. Counts do not establish statistical power, qualitative saturation, validity or the right sample size. Interview participation, missingness, accessibility and participant burden need separate review.

Begin with a question and baseline, identify evidence, label assumptions and uncertainty, compare scenarios and examine sensitivity. Choose relevant dimensions rather than one universal score. Record thresholds, stop conditions and evidence that would change the decision. Delay, revision, no change and reversal remain legitimate possibilities.

A future paid platform may retain scenario versions, connect assessments and analytics, support authorized collaboration and compare conditional projections with observed results. These services are planned, not available here. Technology choice is one optional dimension when relevant. Advertising and Attention is one future application, considering budgets, response, informed choice and long-term trust. No outcome is guaranteed.

Choose what matters to the question

Relevant dimensions, not a universal score.

Users may eventually select financial, marketing, customer, research quality, data quality, operational, human, ethical, accessibility, sustainability, resilience, time, capacity, evidence quality and uncertainty dimensions. Not every scenario requires every category. Keep units, disagreements and tradeoffs visible. Do not combine these into one universal score or automatically declare a winner.

Technology is one possible scenario dimension

When a scenario concerns technology or work design, alternatives may include no technology change, process redesign, traditional software, AI assistance, greater automation, outsourcing, additional human support or a hybrid approach. Fully manual and non-AI approaches remain available considerations.

These options should not appear in every scenario when irrelevant. Human-led work does not require AI. Refusing full delegation does not authorize partial AI assistance. Preserve stated boundaries, practical choice and accountable review.

Limits are part of the method

What Scenario Studio does not do

Not a validated prediction system. Not currently a complete live customer service. Scenario Studio does not automatically:

  • Predict the future.
  • Select the correct marketing strategy.
  • Design a valid research study.
  • Establish causation.
  • Validate user assumptions.
  • Determine qualitative saturation.
  • Determine statistical power without an appropriate method.
  • Guarantee advertising performance.
  • Guarantee research participation.
  • Replace professional or methodological judgment.
  • Convert projections into facts.
  • Establish scientific validation or guarantee business outcomes.
Projections and uncertainty
  • Outputs depend on user-provided assumptions. A projection changes when assumptions change.
  • Uncertainty should remain visible. Missing evidence must not become a confident number.
  • Point estimates are not certainty. Use ranges only with an explained basis.
  • Percentiles require a justified distribution or model. None are calculated here, and no scenario probabilities are assigned.
  • Correlation does not establish causation.
  • A visually precise chart does not make an assumption accurate.

Decision understanding, evidence quality, contextual fit, practical choice, human contributions and burdens, reversibility, sustainability and learning matter. Higher scores, greater technology uptake, lower resistance or purchasing the platform are not success by themselves. Delay, deliberate non-adoption, revision, reversal and no change can be legitimate outcomes.

The public repository retains its current licensing structure. This page does not release the complete local Studio or future commercial backend. Existing published MIT code is not newly confidential. Instruments remain exploratory.

  • Are frequency and channel mix proportionate to the actual need?

  • Who benefits and who carries attention or resource burdens?
